                                                      Abstract: Tracking large human-motion datasets has become the dominant route
                                                      to general, expressive humanoid whole-body control, but scaling across many mo-
                                                      tions is expensive. Current pipelines use model-free reinforcement learning, either
                                                      merging per-motion specialists through lossy distillation, or brute-forcing a single
                                                      policy with cluster-scale compute. SuperMimic amortizes learning across all mo-
                                                      tions with one differentiable world model of their shared dynamics, fit from logged
                                                      transitions of a standard rigid-body simulator. The world model and the tracking
                                                      policy train together in one loop, and backpropagation-through-time over short
                                                      imagined rollouts gives the policy analytic gradients. Fast imagined rollouts and
                                                      analytic gradients make policy learning efficient. In 3 hours on a single GPU, Su-
                                                      perMimic surpasses the return of a state-of-the-art motion tracker baseline trained
                                                      for 7 days on 128 GPUs. It reduces GPU-hours by more than 1000× and fails
                                                      on only 25 of 129,514 benchmark motions, versus 179 failures for the baseline.
                                                      After 9.8 hours on two GPUs, SuperMimic achieves zero failures. From 1 to 264
                                                      hours of motion, the world model’s prediction error stays stable, and GPU-hours
                                                      to a target return grow slowly. Our policy transfers to a physical Unitree G1 across
                                                      diverse and acrobatic motions. SuperMimic removes compute as the bottleneck
                                                      to scaling humanoid whole-body control, making it practical to measure motion-
                                                      tracking scaling laws.

                                         1       Introduction
                                         Humanoid robots hold the promise of operating in environments designed for people, but doing
                                         so requires whole-body controllers that span diverse human motion, from locomotion and dance
                                         to crouching and balance recovery. Physics-based motion imitation addresses this by training RL

policies to track reference motion clips [2] drawn from large motion-capture (mocap) datasets [1].
Existing systems scale this idea either by distilling many clip-specific experts into one controller
[3] or by training a generalist policy over a large motion library [1]. In both cases, a more diverse
motion library produces a more capable controller. But both also become computationally expensive
as motion coverage grows: the state-of-the-art tracker SONIC [1], for example, trains for ∼168 h
on 128 GPUs, totaling roughly 21,000 GPU-hours. Although reference motions differ, they share
the same humanoid hybrid rigid-body dynamics. Model-free RL fails to explicitly model this shared
structure, instead repeatedly estimating action consequences from noisy simulator rollouts. Scaling
to more motions therefore amounts to scaling up compute.
SuperMimic addresses this inefficiency with a single learned, differentiable world model fit from
logged simulator transitions. Backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) through short imagined roll-
outs provides analytic policy gradients through the learned model, allowing one dynamics model to
amortize learning across the entire mocap library. Because these gradients come from the learned
model rather than from differentiating the simulator, SuperMimic remains compatible with rigid-
body simulators, including those with non-smooth contacts and non-differentiable implementations.
On one GPU, SuperMimic surpasses the normalized return of SONIC’s released checkpoint within
3 h and reduces GPU-hours by more than 1000× (§4.1). On a 129,514-motion benchmark, Super-
Mimic fails on 25 motions after 3 h on one GPU and on none after 9.8 h on two GPUs, against 179
for SONIC’s released checkpoint (Appendix F). Across a 264× increase in dataset length, the world
model’s prediction error stays stable and SuperMimic’s compute to a fixed return grows slowly. The
learned policy transfers to a physical Unitree G1. In a preliminary study of downstream interfaces,
an RL-trained velocity encoder outperforms a distillation baseline.


Contributions.
• Model-based RL (MBRL) for deployable humanoid control. We demonstrate that BPTT
  through a learned, differentiable world model can train whole-body tracking policies in a set-
  ting long dominated by model-free proximal policy optimization (PPO) [4], and that the resulting
  controller transfers to a physical Unitree G1.
• Compute is no longer the bottleneck to scaling. SuperMimic surpasses the return of SONIC, a
  cluster-scale PPO pipeline, on a single GPU and reduces GPU-hours by more than 1000× (§4.1).
  Its training cost grows slowly as the motion library expands, making scaling humanoid whole-
  body control practical without huge GPU infrastructure.
• An analysis of the ingredients behind the gains. We identify three world-model properties
  behind the compute reduction: prediction error that stays stable over a 264× increase in dataset
  length, rollouts more than 10× faster than the simulator, and analytic gradients, with which the
  policy reaches the best return of PPO on the world model with about 64× fewer imagined steps.
• RL-trained downstream control beyond distillation. As a preliminary study, the same learned
  world model trains a downstream goal encoder on the interface’s own task reward. On velocity
  commands, the policy tracks more accurately with this encoder than with a distillation baseline.


2   Related Work
Large-scale physics-based motion tracking. Physics-based motion imitation learns controllers that
reproduce reference motions under contact-rich rigid-body dynamics. DeepMimic [2] demonstrated
expressive character skills through model-free reinforcement learning, and PHC [11] extended this
paradigm to large motion sets with robust recovery. SONIC [1] further demonstrated that scaling
motion data, policy capacity, and training compute produces generalist controllers that transfer to
humanoid hardware. These results establish motion tracking as a practical route to broad whole-
body behavior coverage. However, training these large-scale motion trackers requires extensive
simulator rollouts and substantial computation. SuperMimic addresses this bottleneck by learning a
differentiable dynamics model shared across all motions, so policy improvement can exploit analytic
gradients rather than model-free rollout estimates.

Learned differentiable dynamics for motion control. SuperTrack [12] optimizes motion-control
policies through a learned differentiable surrogate of the simulator. ControlVAE [6] jointly learns a
dynamics model, latent skill representation, and generative controller, while MoConVQ [13] extends
learned-model training to discrete motion representations over tens of hours of data. SuperDyno [14]
scales differentiable neural dynamics to tens of thousands of motion clips. These methods establish
the effectiveness of learned dynamics and analytic policy gradients for scalable simulated-character
control. SuperMimic extends this direction to single-GPU training of a large-scale humanoid tracker
and to its sim-to-real deployment: its world model conditions on simulator context, while the de-
ployed actor uses noisy proprioceptive observations and a goal input (§3.2). We evaluate how model
accuracy, rollout throughput, and differentiable policy updates contribute to training efficiency, and
how prediction error behaves as motion coverage grows (§4.1–4.2), and demonstrate physical de-
ployment of the resulting controller (§4.4).
Model-based policy optimization and robot deployment. Dreamer [15] and DreamerV3 [16]
learn latent dynamics for imagination-based actor–critic learning, while TD-MPC [17] combines
short-horizon planning with terminal-value prediction. DayDreamer [18] demonstrates world-model
learning directly on physical robots. SHAC [19] uses differentiable simulation for short-horizon
actor–critic updates, motivating SuperMimic’s critic-bootstrapped objective. Analytic-gradient
robot control can also combine a simplified differentiable surrogate with a high-fidelity forward
simulator, as demonstrated for quadruped locomotion [20]. SuperMimic learns its surrogate from
simulator transitions and backpropagates through short imagined rollouts, retaining compatibility
with standard rigid-body simulators.
RWM [21] and RWM-U [22] demonstrate that policies trained in learned neural simulators can trans-
fer to physical robots. Both optimize policies using PPO on imagined rollouts; RWM-U additionally
uses uncertainty-penalized offline optimization. SuperMimic instead optimizes the policy by differ-
entiating the return through its learned dynamics. RWM and RWM-U pretrain the world model on
trajectories from other policies. In contrast, SuperMimic fits its world model during training on data
its own policy collects.

resentations for downstream reinforcement learning. MaskedMimic [9] supports partial-body and
multimodal goals through motion inpainting, while BeyondMimic [3] uses guided diffusion for goal

3     Method
3.1   SuperMimic training loop

SuperMimic trains one goal-conditioned whole-body controller across a multi-skill motion library
and supports multiple goal interfaces. It captures the shared humanoid dynamics in a differentiable
world model and improves the policy through short imagined rollouts. Training alternates between
three phases in one loop (Figure 2). First, the current policy collects simulator transitions into a
replay buffer. Second, we fit the differentiable world model to short replay windows. Third, the
policy is improved by backpropagating an imagined return through the frozen world model, with
each rollout seeded from a replay-buffer state in the Dyna style [23, 24]. The buffer, model, and
policy are updated throughout training, so dynamics learning and control improve together rather
than in separate stages (Algorithm A.1, Appendix D). Transitions from earlier policies remain valid
for fitting the world model, because the dynamics do not depend on the policy that collected them.
We denote the goal-conditioned partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) by M
and use compact notation with state st , goal gt , and action at . State st gives the proprioceptive
observation ot and the termination dt , which is 1 if st is terminal. Action at in state st yields
the reward rt+1 and the next state st+1 . The policy is deterministic, at = πθ (ot , gt ), and at
parameterizes a normalized joint setpoint; for motion tracking, gt is read from a reference motion.
During data collection, the simulator executes the noisy action at + ϵt with action noise ϵt ∼ ρϵ ,
and the replay buffer stores this executed noisy action. The noise widens the range of actions on
which the world model is fit; it serves only the model fit and

3.2   Learning a differentiable world model
The simulator’s articulated multi-rigid-body dynamics are known, but the simulator need not provide
gradients, so SuperMimic learns a differentiable surrogate to obtain policy gradients: it supplies the
predictions and Jacobians for the BPTT update in §3.3. The resulting analytic policy gradient is the
gradient of a surrogate for the expected return in simulation. Since the simulator’s randomness has
known sources, the world model stays deterministic: it conditions on the per-episode sim context ξ
(friction, calibration offsets, and torso center-of-mass) and predicts the noise-free observation ōt .
The world model contains a learned dynamics model and reward, observation, and termination
heads; each head is either learned or computed analytically,

3.3   Learning the policy through the world model
During each policy update, we hold the world-model weights ω fixed and optimize πθ and Vψ on
imagined rollouts of H=32 steps. The critic Vψ estimates the return beyond step H, so the model
must be accurate only within these H steps. This imagination horizon exceeds the k-step replay
windows on which we fit the model (§3.2). In practice, the learned model remains stable over
indefinitely long rollouts, because the k-step autoregressive fit trains it to correct its own prediction
errors.

The policy and critic updates together form a short-horizon actor–critic in the spirit of SHAC [19],
which backpropagates through a differentiable simulator. SuperMimic replaces SHAC’s hard
episode termination with the survival weight ĉt , and its critic has no exponential-moving-average
target network. Prior motion-tracking controllers trained with analytic gradients often omit a critic
and optimize only the H-step reward sum [12, 6]. Without a critic, training can reach a similar
nominal tracking return, but this unbootstrapped objective is more myopic. The critic matters after
perturbations, where recovery or balance rewards can lie beyond the imagination window (§4.3).


4     Experimental Results

We show that SuperMimic scales favorably relative to PPO pipelines, identify the world-model
properties behind this scaling, demonstrate transfer on a real Unitree G1, and report a preliminary
study of downstream goal interfaces. The simulator is MuJoCo Warp, a GPU implementation of
MuJoCo [26]. All our reported costs are measured on NVIDIA H100 (80 GB SXM5) GPUs of
Lambda on-demand instances. We ran several random seeds and found training very stable, which
we attribute to the dense reward that motion tracking gives at every step. The figures therefore do not
report seed variation. Normalized return, return for short, is the undiscounted sum of the per-step
reward over the full episode, divided by the largest value this sum can take. A policy that terminates
early therefore scores low.


4.1   SuperMimic reaches SONIC-level tracking with far fewer GPU-hours

We compare against SONIC, a published PPO pipeline trained on a 128-GPU cluster. Both methods
build on the BeyondMimic motion-tracking formulation [3], making SONIC the closest available
published comparison. Within 3 h on one GPU, SuperMimic surpasses the normalized return of the
released SONIC checkpoint (Figure 3(a)). On a shared benchmark of 129,514 motions, it also fails
on fewer motions than SONIC: 25 after 3 h on one GPU and 0 after 9.8 h on two GPUs, against
179 for SONIC’s released checkpoint (Appendix F). We state the 3 h conservatively: from then on,
every checkpoint is ahead of SONIC by at least half of the gap between SONIC and SuperMimic’s
best result, in both return and failed motions. SuperMimic thus uses more than 50× less wall-clock
time and more than 1000× fewer GPU-hours than SONIC.
Compute accounting. The ratios compare SONIC’s reported 128 NVIDIA L40 GPUs for 7 days
(about 21,000 GPU-hours) with 3 h on one NVIDIA H100, within which SuperMimic surpasses
the return of SONIC’s released checkpoint. They are about 56× (> 50×) in wall-clock time and
about 7200× (> 1000×) in GPU-hours. The 3 h cover the full training loop; checkpoint evaluations
run after training and are not counted. SuperMimic trains on 264.56 h of motion from BONES-
SEED [27], the public subset of SONIC’s data, about 2.3× less than SONIC’s 611 h. The GPU
models also differ: in peak throughput, an H100 delivers 0.74× the fp32 and 5.5× the tensor-core
performance of an L40; how much of that peak either run used is unknown. Nonetheless, 2.3× in
data and at most 5.5× in peak GPU throughput cannot account for a 1000× gap in GPU-hours.
Figure 4(a) gives a controlled comparison on LAFAN1 [28]. The baseline, PPO-Sim, is PPO trained
in the simulator. PPO-Sim shares SuperMimic’s motions, observations, reward, termination, domain
randomization, actor and critic network sizes, and evaluation protocol. It uses BeyondMimic’s PPO
hyperparameters [3] and 8192 parallel environments per GPU, twice SuperMimic’s. PPO-Sim esti-
mates its policy gradient from sampled simulator rollouts; SuperMimic backpropagates its imagined
return through world-model rollouts. SuperMimic reaches a return of 0.82 in evaluation within 1.2 h
on one GPU. Within the 18 h budget, PPO-Sim does not reach it. Its final return is 0.60 on one GPU,
0.70 on four, and 0.72 on eight, so going from four to eight GPUs barely helps.
Figure 3(b, c) shows that, for SuperMimic, GPU-hours to the target return grow slowly as dataset
length increases, while on the same sweep the world model’s prediction error stays stable; §4.2
traces both trends to one world model amortizing the dynamics shared across motions.

4.2       Properties of the learned world model

These results rest on three properties of the learned world model fω : its accuracy holds as the motion
library grows, and its rollouts are fast and differentiable.
One model amortizes the motion library. In Figure 3(c), the world model’s 8-step prediction error
stays between 0.007 and 0.011 as the dataset grows from 1 to 264 h. Every motion is executed by the
same humanoid, so added clips supply more evidence about the shared body and contact dynamics
rather than demanding new capacity. This is consistent with the slow growth of the compute needed
to reach the target return (§4.1).
Imagination accelerates rollouts. During policy optimization, rollout speed sets the update cost.
Figure 5(a) shows that the world model runs more than 10× faster than MuJoCo Warp at the batch
sizes used in practice, and up to 62× at 64k parallel environments.

4.3       A critic enables shorter horizons and push recovery

Figure 4(b, c) sweeps H with and without the critic. Without the critic, training fails at H ≤ 16.
With it, H=16 matches the critic-free H=32, and H=32 reaches a return of 0.82 fastest. Raising H
from 64 to 128 nearly triples the time to that return and lowers the final return; H=128 is the longest
horizon that fits on an H100. At matched return, the critic still changes behavior. Under 0.5–4 m/s
velocity pushes while holding a static T-pose in simulation, the critic policy settles within 0.5–2.4 s.
The critic-free policy never settles: it keeps stumbling even at 0.5 m/s and falls at 4 m/s.

4.4       Real-robot transfer

One general motion tracker deploys on a real Unitree G1 without per-motion tuning (Figure 6). The
four motions cover locomotion (a skipping jump), contact-rich whole-body motion with the torso
and limbs on the floor (a side roll), and acrobatics (a 90◦ flip and an in-place 360◦ flip).

4.5       Downstream tasks: RL encoder vs. distillation baseline

SuperMimic trains downstream goal encoders jointly with the motion-tracking interface, sharing one
decoder and world model (Appendix B). Our study of downstream interfaces is preliminary. We train
one velocity encoder on a velocity-tracking reward through the world model. We compare it with a
distillation baseline: a velocity encoder trained on the motion-tracking reward, with commands read
from the reference motions. Figure 7 evaluates both on 672 step responses spanning the velocity
space (Appendix G). The RL encoder has the lower steady-state error on 650 of them, and the
distillation encoder on 15. Only the distillation encoder falls, on seven fast turns with almost no
motion-library data.

5        Conclusion
SuperMimic removes compute as the bottleneck to scaling humanoid whole-body control. A
single learned, differentiable world model captures the dynamics every motion shares, and
backpropagation-through-time through it amortizes a whole dataset into one policy. The policy
trains on a single GPU, surpasses the return of a policy trained on a 128-GPU cluster, and transfers
to a real Unitree G1. Its training cost grows slowly over a controlled dataset-length sweep. The

6       Limitations and Future Work
• Multi-interface training is understudied. We train the velocity encoder concurrently with the
  motion tracker, but the velocity-tracking reward updates only the velocity encoder and not the
  shared decoder (Appendix B). Concurrent training is therefore the same as training the velocity
  encoder after the motion tracker, and might not be necessary. Whether concurrent training helps
  once the velocity-tracking reward also updates the decoder is untested.
• Simulator-only dynamics. The world model learns only from simulator transitions. Real-robot
  data could refine it, and its differentiability could support system identification.
• No terrain or object interaction. SuperMimic trains only on flat ground. Extending it to terrain
  and object interaction is ongoing work.

frames. For an upright reference, etilt reaches 0.8 at about 78◦ of tilt. Unlike BeyondMimic, we do
not terminate on end-effector deviation.

Algorithm A.1 summarizes the SuperMimic training loop of §3.1; its three steps correspond one-to-

Return-based reset sampling. Simulator episodes reset to points of the motion library, favor-
ing those where the policy currently earns low return. The motion timeline is divided into 1 s
bins, and each bin b keeps a running estimate Gb of the discounted return from it, updated on
the policy’s own rollouts. Resets mix three distributions: 50% uniform over bins, 40% from
softmaxb (−25 Gb /Gmax ), where Gmax is the largest attainable discounted return, and 10% in pro-
portion to each bin’s past softmax weight, so a hard motion keeps some practice after it is learned.

       • Elevated support. zmin holds above 0.20 m for at least 0.6 s (standing or climbing on a
         box).

       • Ground penetration. zmin falls below −0.12 m, or below −0.08 m for over 80% of the
         clip (legs driven through the floor by a faulty retarget).

       • Floating feet. For at least 0.5 s both feet stay more than 0.02 m above the floor—measured
         as MuJoCo’s exact geom-to-plane distance (mj geomDistance) from the foot collision
         geometry, not a link-origin proxy—while the pelvis is upright and at standing height and
         the body is nearly stationary. A free-standing humanoid cannot hold both feet off the floor
         while standing still, so this is infeasible on flat ground whatever the cause (a low platform,
         a retarget float, or a slow rise; a ballistic jump is excluded by the stationary gate). The clean
         ground stance sits at −0.004 ± 0.004 m, so the 0.02 m gate is several σ clear of the floor.

F     Evaluation
F.1   Benchmark metrics

We evaluate SONIC and SuperMimic on the same filtered BONES-SEED training-set benchmark,
containing 129,514 evaluated motions. For SONIC we evaluate its released checkpoint and its v1.1
checkpoint [1]. Each motion plays once from its first frame, with the same simulation for every
policy: foot friction 1.0, no domain randomization or observation noise, and deterministic physics.
Each policy uses its own actuator gains and action scale, and receives its observations as its training
environment builds them. A motion is marked as failed if the rollout triggers SuperMimic’s training
termination (Appendix A). SONIC’s own success criterion fails a motion on root and end-effector
height deviations above 0.25 m, without an orientation term [1].

G       Velocity-command task
G.1     Training setup

The velocity encoder of §4.5 maps the command (vx , vy , ωz ) and a feature of the last 10 frames of
o to the shared latent.
Each encoder is trained from scratch in its own run, jointly with the motion-tracking interface, for
100,000 iterations. Both runs use a walking and running library filtered from LAFAN1 and BONES-
SEED. Half of the 4096 simulator environments and half of each imagined batch use the velocity
interface, so p(I) is uniform; unlike Algorithm A.1, which samples one interface per update, each
imagined batch mixes both.
Commands. The command gt = (vx , vy , ωz ) stacks the target planar root velocity in the heading
(yaw-only) frame and the target world-frame yaw rate. A reference command is the centered 25-
frame mean of the reference root velocity in the reference’s heading frame. For the RL encoder,
each environment resamples its command source at every reset and every 250 control steps (5 s):
with probability 0.5 it reads the command from the reference clip at every step, and otherwise it
holds a command drawn uniformly from vx ∈ [−1.5, 2] m/s, vy ∈ [−1.32, 1.32] m/s and ωz ∈
[−3.35, 3.35] rad/s, zeroed (standing) with probability 0.1.


                                                     18
Encoder. On rollouts with reference commands, a mean-squared loss of weight 0.1 pulls the velocity
latent toward the motion encoder’s latent at the same step, with a stop-gradient on the motion latent.
Rollouts, reward, and termination. Each interface’s imagined rollouts start from states logged
by its own simulator environments. Velocity rollouts of the RL encoder use the velocity-tracking
reward and a fall-only termination (Table A.6); those of the distillation baseline use the motion-
tracking reward and termination of Appendix A.

G.2   Step test
Protocol. From standing, the policy receives a fixed command for 5 s; each command is tested in
four trials. The relative steady-state error is the norm of the mean velocity error over the last 2 s,
divided by the norm of the command, with the root velocity measured in the heading frame. The
per-command value is the median over the trials that did not fall. The 672 step responses form a
15 × 15 grid on each of three planes, spanning the uniform command range above, with the standing
command left out. In Figure 7, errors above 0.9 are drawn at 0.9, and a policy that fell in all four
trials is drawn at the cap.
Results. The RL encoder has the lower error on 650 of the 672 step responses, and the distillation
encoder on 15. Their median relative errors are 0.16 and 0.49. Distillation falls in every trial on seven
commands, all combining translation with a turn of at least 2.8 rad/s, where the motion library has
almost no data. Each policy has one training seed.
